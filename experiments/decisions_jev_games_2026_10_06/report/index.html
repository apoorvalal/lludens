Individual payoffs and joint efficiency in repeated games

GPT-6 Luna and Jev 1.13.0 play twenty-round Prisoner’s Dilemma, Stag Hunt, and two-player public-goods games. Each player receives the full realized history and makes a fresh API decision in every round. The action returned by the API is played directly.

In sixteen head-to-head matches per game, Jev earns a higher mean individual payoff in all three games. Neither pairing achieves sustained joint cooperation: mean joint payoffs reach 36.0%, 73.5%, and 62.7% of their respective feasible maxima. None of the 48 head-to-head matches attains a Pareto-efficient cumulative payoff vector. These are distinct findings about individual returns and collective outcomes.

Information and experimental design

Each match has two players, the same opponent throughout, and exactly twenty simultaneous rounds. Both players know the payoff rules and the final round. Each is instructed to maximize its own expected total match payoff, including future rounds—not its lead over the opponent or the joint score. There is no discounting, communication, side payment, or reward outside the match.

Before round t, each player receives:

  • The complete game rules, legal actions, its player number, current round t, and horizon 20.
  • Every completed round 1,…,t−1: its own action, the other player’s action, and both realized payoffs, expressed from its own perspective.
  • Its cumulative points so far. The other player’s cumulative points can be calculated from the supplied history.

The opponent’s identity and strategy are unknown. Neither current-round move is disclosed until both have been selected. The application supplies memory explicitly in a new request every round; no persistent conversation, private reasoning history, or cross-match learning is supplied. Thus a match contains 40 fresh decisions—20 per player. The state changes after every realized action pair. This protocol permits history-dependent behavior; it does not establish which strategy a model implements.

The primary panel contains 48 head-to-head matches: 3 games × 8 seat-swapped pairs × 2 seat assignments (960 rounds; 1,920 decisions). Legal-option order is shuffled by seat and round. Seat swaps preserve the seat-specific shuffle schedule; the payoff explanation remains fixed. Seeds control this schedule, not service-side randomness. Twelve direct-action self-play matches are reported separately below. Within-match rounds are dependent observations, not independent experimental replications.

Games, payoffs, and efficiency benchmarks

Let uᵢₜ denote player i’s round-t points, Uᵢ = ∑ₜ₌₁²⁰ uᵢₜ its cumulative payoff, and J = U₁ + U₂ the joint cumulative payoff. All scores below are in the game’s stated points; scores are not pooled across different games.

Prisoner’s Dilemma

Actions are Cooperate (C) and Defect (D). Entries are (player 1 points, player 2 points).

Player 1 / Player 2CD
C(3, 3)(0, 5)
D(5, 0)(1, 1)

Defection is strictly dominant in the stage game. Twenty rounds of mutual defection give (20, 20); twenty rounds of mutual cooperation give (60, 60) and maximize J at 120.

Stag Hunt

Player 1 / Player 2StagHare
Stag(4, 4)(0, 3)
Hare(3, 0)(3, 3)

Stag/Stag and Hare/Hare are both stage-game equilibria. Hare guarantees 3; Stag earns more in expectation only if the probability of the other choosing Stag exceeds ¾. Twenty rounds of Hare/Hare give (60, 60); Stag/Stag throughout gives (80, 80), the unique Pareto-efficient cumulative vector, and J = 160.

Two-player public goods

Each player receives a fresh endowment of 10 tokens every round and contributes cᵢ ∈ {0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10}. The pool is multiplied by 1.6 and split equally. Unspent endowment does not carry forward, but points accumulate:

uᵢ = 10 − cᵢ + 0.8(cᵢ + cⱼ),   uⱼ = 10 − cⱼ + 0.8(cᵢ + cⱼ).
Joint round payoff: uᵢ + uⱼ = 20 + 0.6(cᵢ + cⱼ).

Zero contribution is a strictly dominant stage-game action: each contributed token reduces own payoff by 0.2 and increases the other player’s payoff by 0.8. Both contribute zero: (10, 10); both contribute ten: (16, 16); contributions (0, 10): (18, 8). Over twenty rounds, zero contributions give (200, 200), whereas full contributions give (320, 320) and maximize J at 640.

What counts as joint attainment?

Prisoner’s DilemmaC / C(60, 60)120
Stag HuntStag / Stag(80, 80)160
Two-player public goods10 / 10(320, 320)640

These are jointly feasible, symmetric, Pareto-efficient benchmarks, not separate individual payoff ceilings. “Attain J*” counts matches reaching the indicated vector, which requires the joint-maximizing pair in all twenty rounds. “J / J*” measures the share of the maximum joint points earned; it is neither an attainment frequency nor the fraction of matches that are Pareto-efficient.

A payoff vector is Pareto-efficient if no feasible alternative makes one player better off without making the other worse off. The PD and public-goods frontiers also contain asymmetric vectors: for example, permanent D against C yields (100, 0), and permanent contributions (0, 10) yield (360, 160). These are efficient but do not maximize J. The analysis checks the full feasible cumulative frontier separately from attainment of the symmetric benchmark. All observed head-to-head vectors lie strictly below their symmetric benchmark for both players, so every one is Pareto-dominated.

Interpretation and limits


The experiment distinguishes earning more than the opponent from jointly attaining an efficient outcome. Jev has the higher head-to-head mean in each game, mainly because Luna sometimes bears the cost of unilateral cooperation or the risk of unmatched Stag play. This does not establish that Jev learns or reasons better, or that either service implements a particular repeated-game strategy. The trajectories alone do not identify tit-for-tat, beliefs, or a planning mechanism.


The cooperative payoff vectors are welfare benchmarks, not the players’ instructed objective and not necessarily equilibria. In the known finite-horizon PD and public-goods games, standard backward induction under common knowledge of rationality selects defection and zero contribution. The prompts do not assert that the opponent satisfies those assumptions. Low cooperation is not itself evidence of a decision error. In Stag Hunt, Hare/Hare is an equilibrium despite being Pareto-dominated by Stag/Stag.


Results are descriptive and conditional on these model versions, one framing, one payoff specification per game, a known twenty-round horizon, and no communication. Eight seat-swapped pairs per game do not support a broad model ranking. Public goods has two players; no claim about larger groups follows. Uncertainty across prompts, horizons, opponents, or repeated service calls was not estimated here.

Scope, provenance, and offline reproduction

The report uses all 48 direct head-to-head matches and separately reports all 12 direct self-play matches. The archive additionally retains 24 matches with experimenter-imposed action randomization and 166 diagnostic queries; neither enters the result tables or replay above. Randomizing a returned response distribution changes the agent and is not a second estimate of the same model comparison.


All original requests and recorded trajectories are retained. The offline audit checks the complete archive; direct-action accounting independently recomputes payoffs and the feasible cumulative Pareto frontier with exact rational arithmetic. The original all-panel data/summary.json is preserved for audit. This memo uses data/direct_summary.json, also served here as summary.json.


Successful calls were journaled individually and interrupted matches resumed from their saved histories. Twelve initially rejected probability-validation responses (including one diagnostic) were not retained and had to be requested again; they did not become fallback actions. This limits reconstruction of the rejected attempts, not the retained game paths. Full integration provenance and pinned plugin versions are in the experiment README.
